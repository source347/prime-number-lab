Why factorisation matters

360 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 = 2³ × 3² × 5

The fundamental theorem of arithmetic says every integer greater than 1 has exactly one prime factorisation, apart from the order of its factors. Primes are the irreducible building blocks of the positive integers.

The Prime Number Theorem says π(x) ~ x/ln(x). The logarithmic integral Li(x) is usually a closer smooth approximation. Neither is an exact prime counter.

What the approximation means

π(x) ~ Li(x) ~ x / ln(x)   as x → ∞

The symbol ~ means the ratio tends to 1 as x grows. It does not mean the curves are identical. Their difference contains information about the irregularity of prime distribution.

Euler’s product formula

The zeta function can be expressed as a product over every prime.

Why this is remarkable

Each prime contributes one factor. Multiplying the contributions of all primes reconstructs the zeta function in its region of convergence.

1/(1 − p−s) = 1 + p−s + p−2s + …

Expanding the product generates terms corresponding to unique prime factorisations. That is Euler’s bridge between primes and the zeta series.

The convergent Euler product requires Re(s) > 1. It cannot be used as a convergent product on the critical line Re(s) = 1/2.

RSA: primes in cryptography

A small arithmetic demonstration of public-key encryption.

Choose two primes

What is happening?

RSA chooses two primes p and q, multiplies them to obtain n = pq, and uses modular arithmetic to construct public and private keys.

n = pq
φ(n) = (p − 1)(q − 1)
c ≡ me (mod n)
m ≡ cd (mod n)
These tiny demonstration keys are completely insecure. Real RSA requires large random primes, secure padding and vetted cryptographic software. Never use this demo to protect data.
